The Role of Myth

Myth in Human History

Human history is beautifully complex, shaped by migration, cultural exchange, shared ancestry, and the environments in which societies develop. Myths preserve this complexity by revealing how societies made sense of themselves and the world around them. These stories offer a rare window into intangible cultural heritage, carrying the values, memories, and ways of understanding that formal historical records often overlook. To study myths, therefore, is to trace the deeper history of human thought and the evolving relationship between people, their communities, and their surroundings.

These ways of understanding become visible in the stories themselves. In one ancient Greek tradition, the seasonal decline and renewal of vegetation took narrative form in Demeter’s grief during Persephone’s absence and the earth’s revival upon her return. A Korean eclipse tale interpreted the sudden darkening of the sun or moon through the Bulgae, fire dogs from a realm of darkness whose attempts to seize the celestial bodies temporarily obscured their light. In many Māori creation traditions, the emergence of the natural world belongs within a genealogy: when Tāne separated Ranginui, the sky father, from Papatūānuku, the earth mother, light entered the world, and their descendants became associated with its forests, seas, and other natural domains. Taken together, these examples show how myth can make the cosmos socially meaningful, connecting environmental cycles and celestial events to human experiences of kinship, separation, struggle, and renewal.

These examples demonstrate that myths are not isolated explanations of the world, but cultural records continually shaped as people migrate, encounter other communities, and adapt inherited stories to new environments. Similarities among traditions may preserve traces of shared ancestry or historical exchange, while their differences reveal how societies reinterpret common ideas through distinct cultural experiences. This tension between continuity and transformation has made myth an important subject of historical and anthropological comparison. Previous research has therefore examined how geography, language, and cultural contact influence the distribution of stories—and how these overlapping forces complicate efforts to reconstruct their origins and relationships.

Because myths carry a community’s memory, preserving them is also an act of preserving culture. Yet oral traditions endure only when they are remembered, retold, recorded, or collected. As generations change and languages, rituals, and social settings transform, stories may be reshaped, fragmented, or lost altogether. Archives and catalogues therefore serve as bridges between living cultural memory and historical study, allowing traces of traditions to survive beyond the people and moments that produced them.

“Folklore is the boiled-down juice of human living.”

Zora Neale Hurston, writing in “Folklore and Music”

Preservation and cataloguing

Preservation, however, is never a neutral or complete process.

Every surviving tablet, vessel, printed image, or transcription reflects a sequence of choices about what was recorded, copied, collected, translated, classified, and retained.

Although each medium transmits part of a narrative across time, none can fully reproduce the social world in which it once circulated—the performance, audience, ritual setting, relationships, and cultural authority that gave it meaning. Catalogues extend this process by making recurrence visible across collections too vast for any single reader to comprehend, but they do so by reducing complex traditions into retrievable categories. They therefore preserve not living cultures in their entirety, but mediated traces of cultural memory whose value depends upon careful interpretation of both what they contain and what they leave outside their frame.

These objects are historical counterpoints, not samples from the 926 catalogue records, representatives of analytical groups, or stages in a single civilizational sequence. They show what a motif matrix necessarily leaves outside its frame: material, genre, audience, use, and authority.

History of Catalogues

History of Cataloging Global Mythology

These objects show how stories can survive long after the moments in which they were first told. But preserving a narrative is only the first step.

As collections grew across languages, regions, and historical periods, scholars also needed a way to bring scattered stories into relation with one another and to recognize when similar plots or narrative elements appeared in different traditions. Cataloguing developed in response to that need. By organizing stories into recurring tale types and motifs, scholars made large bodies of folklore searchable and comparable. Over time, these systems moved from classifying whole plots to indexing smaller narrative elements and, eventually, mapping their distribution through digital databases. Each stage expanded what researchers could study, while also shaping the particular forms of similarity that became visible.

  1. 1910

    Tale types become classifiable

    Antti Aarne formalizes recurring plots

    Aarne’s Verzeichnis der Märchentypen assigned stable numbers to recurring plots, giving scholars a shared comparative language. Its evidence base remained largely European.

  2. 1928 and 1961

    The tale-type system expands

    Stith Thompson internationalizes Aarne’s index

    Thompson expanded Aarne’s catalogue in 1928 and revised it in 1961, broadening its bibliography and geographic reach while retaining the tale-type framework.

  3. 1932–1958

    Motifs become searchable

    Thompson indexes smaller narrative elements

    Thompson’s six-volume Motif-Index (1932–36; revised 1955–58) made smaller elements—such as earth-divers, stolen fire, and supernatural birth—traceable across different stories.

  4. 1993 onward

    Cataloguing becomes globally analytical

    Yuri Berezkin develops a tradition-by-motif database

    Berezkin computerized the catalogue in 1993, began statistical analysis the next year, and expanded it across the Americas and Old World. Its tradition-by-motif matrix supports research on geographic distribution and historical contact.

03 The Problem of Representation

Every catalogue carries a point of view, and that point of view has consequences.

To classify is to choose a name, a unit, a language, and a boundary. Those choices make comparison possible in the first place; they also decide, in advance, which parts of a narrative will remain visible to everyone who comes later.

A catalogue record is not a transparent window onto an encounter. Names, captions, translations, and metadata frame what a later reader is able to retrieve and, just as importantly, what that reader believes they are seeing. Metadata does not simply label evidence; it interprets it. A motif title performs a comparable reduction, because it cannot carry tone, gesture, audience, disagreement, sacred restriction, or a teller’s reason for speaking, and because the editorial language of the catalogue—its choices of English wording—enters the record alongside the narrative it describes. What becomes searchable is genuinely valuable, but it is never identical to the source narrative or to the occasion of its performance.

“Each standard and each category valorizes some point of view and silences another.”

— Geoffrey C. Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, Sorting Things Out

This is why a single technical detail of the dataset carries so much interpretive weight. A blank cell in the matrix means only that a given motif was not recorded in this frozen export; it does not prove that a community lacks the narrative, and uneven documentation must never be read as evidence that one tradition is richer, more complex, or more imaginative than another. The networks built in this study organise records within an archive; they do not classify cultures, and a motif remains a trace rather than the story itself.

Far from being a reason to abandon comparison, these limits sharpen the question the research is designed to answer. If representation shapes what we see, then the honest question is not “what is the true shape of world mythology?” but something more testable: which patterns survive when the meaning of similarity itself is changed?

04 Current Works and Gaps in Research

Computational Folkloristics

Large-scale comparative research requires myths to be translated into a form that can be systematically analyzed across cultures. Motif catalogues make this possible by reducing complex narratives into recurring elements—such as the theft of fire, the emergence of land from water, or a journey between worlds—and recording where each element appears. This framework has enabled scholars to compare large collections of records and identify broad patterns associated with geography, population history, and cultural transmission. The frozen Berezkin Catalogue export analyzed here records 2,138 motif IDs across 926 heterogeneous catalogue records, providing an unusually extensive foundation for global analysis.

Yet motif codes also define cultural similarity within relatively rigid categorical boundaries. Two traditions are treated as related when they share an identical catalogued motif, but not necessarily when they express a similar underlying idea through different characters, events, or symbols. One tradition might explain human mortality through a broken prohibition, while another describes a failed attempt at resurrection; although both confront the irreversibility of death, they may share no formal motif code. The absence of direct overlap, therefore, does not necessarily indicate the absence of a deeper conceptual relationship.

This distinction creates an important methodological limitation. A motif-based network measures documented categorical overlap, but it cannot fully represent the broader meanings connecting stories across cultural forms. Its structure may reflect not only historical transmission, but also decisions about how motifs were defined, translated, separated, or grouped within the catalogue. Motif analysis remains especially valuable for tracing the distribution of specific narrative elements, yet it may overlook relationships preserved at a higher level of abstraction—such as shared ideas of cosmic order, transformation, boundary crossing, sacrifice, or renewal.

Computational approaches such as topic modeling offer a potential way to recover some of these less explicit relationships by identifying recurring patterns across narrative descriptions rather than requiring exact code matches. Previous studies have applied topic models and other semantic methods to discover latent structures within folktale collections, demonstrating that computational models can complement established folkloristic classifications. However, these methods have generally been applied to individual corpora or treated separately from conventional motif analysis, leaving limited understanding of how motif-based and semantic representations organize the same global body of mythology differently.

05 Data and Methodology

Methodology

A resemblance is a pattern to explain, not a history already proven. Resemblance between catalogue records may reflect shared ancestry, historical contact and borrowing, the movement of people, a common environment that invites similar stories, recurring concerns of human life, or some combination. Because cultural records are not independent, similarity is never self-explaining. A network cannot decide among these histories; it can identify patterns robust enough to deserve further explanation.

To make that comparison controlled, the study fixes one frozen English-language export of Yuri Berezkin’s catalogue: 926 heterogeneous catalogue records, 2,138 motif IDs, and the recorded assignments connecting them. The same rows and source assignments enter both analyses. The exact-motif pipeline links records that share motif IDs, retaining every positive Jaccard connection. The thematic pipeline derives fifteen provisional factors from English motif titles and descriptions, represents each record as a factor profile, and builds a sparser twelve-neighbor cosine network. Each network receives a separately selected Leiden setting appropriate to its structure.

The resulting four exact-motif groups and five thematic groups are groups of catalogue records—not peoples, identities, or complete traditions. Comparative analysis asks where the two complete pipelines agree, whether that agreement exceeds chance, where the principal split occurs, and how that split changes when broad geography and documentation depth are considered. Because representation, graph construction, and community-detection settings all differ, no difference can be attributed to representation alone. The analysis generates hypotheses; it does not prove migration, ancestry, contact, borrowing, origin, environmental cause, or direction of transmission.

06 Results

One Catalogue, Two Network Representations

Results overview

Key findings

  • One archive provides two complementary views. The same 926 records form four exact-motif groups and five thematic groups.
  • The networks share a clear common structure. Their agreement is substantially greater than expected by chance, and three large community patterns appear in both.
  • The largest exact-motif group shows the principal reorganization. Its records are arranged mainly into a sky and world-order profile and a first-beings and land profile.
  • The broad thematic pattern is the most stable result. Individual motif-level estimates vary with broad geography and documentation depth.

Interpretive scope: the findings establish reproducible patterns within the frozen English-language catalogue and identify priorities for further research. Historical attribution—including migration, borrowing, common ancestry, contact, environmental influence, origin or homeland, and transmission direction—is evaluated through independent contextual evidence.

Network representations

The exact-motif and thematic networks operationalize resemblance at complementary analytical levels.

The two representations examine the same frozen archive at complementary levels. The exact-motif network uses recorded motif identifiers, while the thematic network uses aggregate lexical-semantic emphasis inferred from English catalogue descriptions. Their comparison evaluates structures that recur across the two complete analytical pipelines and organization specific to each pipeline.

In the exact-motif network, resemblance is measured by Jaccard similarity: the number of motif identifiers shared by two records divided by the number present in either record. “Primeval sky close to earth” and “Theft of fire” remain distinct catalogue elements, and a shared connection arises when the same motif identifier is recorded in both records. This representation preserves catalogue-coded distinctions and measures recorded motif overlap. Original wording, performance, and complete narratives remain dimensions for separate source-based analysis.

In the thematic network, an unsupervised model condenses English motif titles and glosses into fifteen recurring lexical-semantic factors. Provisional examples include “Sun, Moon and eclipses,” “first people, plants and beings,” and “water, fish and aquatic beings.” Each record receives a profile across the fifteen factors, and similarity between those profiles determines network connections. The factor labels function as provisional analytical summaries; human validation and community-grounded interpretation provide separate sources of cultural meaning.

Leiden community detection identifies four connected communities in the exact-motif pipeline and five communities in the thematic pipeline. These communities describe organizational emphases among catalogue records within the frozen archive.

Cross-pipeline agreement

The two networks exhibit statistically significant structural agreement.

Three large community correspondences recur under both representations, while a fourth is reorganized across pipelines. The adjusted Rand index is 0.3549 and adjusted mutual information is 0.3888. The networks coassign 55,389 pairs of records, compared with 26,625.23 expected under fixed group sizes, yielding an analytical z-Rand score of 151.11. The observed agreement exceeded every value from 99,999 global permutations (p = 0.00001). In the region-stratified permutation test, the mean adjusted Rand index increased to 0.2075, and the observed value again exceeded the complete permuted distribution. This region-stratified null permits greater expected agreement than the global null, while the observed agreement remains above its full distribution.

Within this frozen archive, the two pipelines preserve common large-scale organization alongside meaningful local reclassification. Because the complete pipelines differ in representation, graph construction, and community-detection settings, the design evaluates convergence and pipeline-specific organization at the level of the complete pipelines.

Principal thematic reorganization

B1 is reorganized primarily into sky and world-order and first-beings and land profiles.

The principal reorganization is concentrated within B1, the largest exact-motif group, which contains 363 records. Under the thematic representation, 162 of these records enter T2 and 148 enter T4; the remaining 53 are distributed among T1, T3, and T5. The dominant correspondence therefore comprises two branches of unequal size with residual allocation across three additional groups. The concentration of primeval-water and earth-diver motifs belongs primarily to the separate B4–T1 correspondence and characterizes a distinct part of the cross-tabulation.

The clearest B1 contrast occurs at the thematic-factor level. Records entering T2 show greater relative emphasis on “Sun, Moon and eclipses” and “sky, earth and world structure.” Records entering T4 show greater emphasis on “first people, plants and beings” and “trees, rocks and climbing.” Four factor differences remain after labels are shuffled within broad-macroarea and motif-count strata. These internal contrasts characterize the thematic partition; independent validation remains a separate research step. Their evidentiary scope is variation in English catalogue language, with population-level interpretation situated in historical and community-grounded research.

Geography and documentation depth provide further context. Ninety of the 148 B1 records entering T4 are located in the catalogue’s combined macroarea of Amazonia and Northern South America, whereas B1 records entering T2 are distributed across several macroareas. B1∩T2 records contain an average of 35.3 recorded motifs per record, compared with 61.1 for B1∩T4. Thematic emphasis, geographic composition, and documentation depth therefore covary within this comparison.

An initial record-level motif comparison identified 232 differences. Adjustment for broad geography retained eight, and the joint specification for region and documentation depth retained zero independently identified differences. Across these sensitivity analyses, the aggregate thematic contrast demonstrates greater stability, while motif-level estimates remain context-sensitive. The network results define priorities for historical, linguistic, archaeological, ecological, ethnographic, and community-grounded research.

Methodological implications

Representation and graph construction contribute to the observed network structure.

The representation of a cultural archive is an analytical decision that, together with the graph-construction procedure, defines the form of similarity available for measurement. The paired design evaluates this dependence directly by identifying both structure that recurs across pipelines and local organization associated with a particular pipeline.

Substantial aggregate agreement coexists with consequential classification differences, making sites of local reorganization priorities for further study. The exact-motif and thematic networks represent two analytical levels of the same cultural record: documented catalogue distinctions and aggregate lexical-semantic emphases inferred from English descriptions. Their evidentiary scope is the organization of archived records and descriptions, while cultural interpretation proceeds through historical, linguistic, ethnographic, and community-grounded inquiry.

The study provides a reproducible model for computational comparison in the humanities. It evaluates structures that remain stable across two specified pipelines, identifies pipeline-specific organization, distinguishes robust aggregate contrasts from context-sensitive motif-level results, and incorporates audits of geography and documentation depth into historical interpretation. The evidence supports comparison of the two methods and establishes analysis across multiple specified representations as a means of estimating the dependence of substantive findings on analytical choices.

Related analyses: thematic-factor contrasts, geographic composition, group profiles, climate context, and latitude distributions.

07 · What it means for society

Similarity without sameness. Difference without isolation.

This research shows that the structure of global mythology changes with the lens through which it is viewed. A network built from exact motif identifiers preserves specific, documented correspondences between traditions. A network built from broader thematic relationships reveals connections that remain invisible when similarity requires identical catalogue codes. Their differences are not merely technical discrepancies; they demonstrate that cultural relationship is not a single property waiting to be uncovered. It is partly shaped by how narratives are recorded, represented, and compared.

01 What the comparison reveals

The study’s central contribution is therefore not the claim that one representation is more truthful than the other. Exact motifs retain the historical specificity and scholarly provenance of the catalogue, while thematic modeling can recover conceptual relationships expressed through different figures, events, and symbols. Read together, the two networks distinguish durable patterns from those that depend on a particular analytical framework. Their points of agreement provide stronger evidence of recurring cultural structure, while their disagreements identify traditions and relationships that require closer historical, linguistic, and ethnographic interpretation.

02 How to read the patterns

For folklore and anthropology, this approach suggests that computational comparison is most valuable when it makes its own limits visible. Large-scale models can reveal geographic patterns, unexpected cultural proximities, and possible pathways of transmission that no individual scholar could recognize across thousands of records. Yet these patterns should be treated as questions rather than conclusions. A network cannot determine whether similarity arose through common ancestry, migration, cultural contact, parallel adaptation, translation, or classification. It can show where a relationship deserves attention; explaining that relationship still requires historical context and engagement with the communities whose traditions are represented.

03 Why this matters beyond mythology

This principle extends beyond mythology. Archives, museums, search engines, educational platforms, and artificial-intelligence systems increasingly mediate how cultural knowledge is organized and encountered. Every system of retrieval makes judgments about what belongs together, what counts as equivalent, and which distinctions should be preserved. Systems that recognize only exact matches may conceal meaningful relationships, while systems that generalize too quickly may dissolve culturally specific knowledge into interchangeable content. Responsible cultural technologies must therefore preserve provenance, acknowledge uncertainty, and allow multiple forms of relationship to coexist rather than presenting one classification as definitive.

04 What computation is for

Ultimately, this research reframes computational analysis not as a replacement for humanistic interpretation, but as a means of enlarging it. The purpose of mapping mythology is not to reduce the world’s traditions to a single universal structure. It is to make previously unseen relationships available for deeper inquiry while preserving the differences that give those relationships meaning.

A catalogue turns stories into comparable traces; computation reveals patterns among them. But the value of those patterns lies not in replacing the worlds from which the stories came, but in helping us return to those worlds with better questions.
