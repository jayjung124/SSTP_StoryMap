Living stories, cultural memory, and the politics of comparison

The Shape of Myth

Myths help people place human life within a larger world. They give origins, obligations, danger, loss, nature, and mortality forms that can be remembered and passed on. This story follows living narratives as they change, enter archives, and become comparable data—then asks what two readings of the same 926 catalogue records reveal and conceal.

Yaejoon (Jay) Jung · Independent research project

01

What myth does now

Myth in the present tense

“Folklore is the boiled-down juice of human living.”

— Zora Neale Hurston, “Folklore and Music”

Myth is not only a story about the past. It is a way of living in the present.

In different communities, narratives grouped by scholars under the word myth may be sacred histories, teachings, genealogies, songs, warnings, or accounts named in their own languages. They can connect people to ancestors and landscapes, carry knowledge and values, and make questions of origin, responsibility, suffering, and mortality discussable.

No single function belongs to every story. Meaning arises in use: in who tells a narrative, who may hear it, when it is performed, what relationships it sustains, and with whose authority it circulates.

The Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage describes folklore and folklife as expressive culture learned orally, by imitation, or in performance. That broader frame helps explain how narratives circulate, but myth, folklore, and community-specific genres are not interchangeable names. Myth matters not because it is old, but because people continue to make life with it.

Orientation

To place life inside a larger world

Stories can relate human beings to water, sky, animals, plants, ancestors, and the unknown. They make a landscape more than scenery and turn the conditions of life into relationships that can be remembered.

Continuity

To remember and belong

Narratives can carry collective memory across generations, link the living with those who came before, and give identity and belonging a language. What survives is not only information, but a way of recognizing oneself in relation to others.

Reflection

To live with obligation, danger, and loss

Stories make difficult questions shareable. Kinship, deception, transformation, conflict, and death become events with causes and consequences—materials through which people can teach, deliberate, mourn, and imagine otherwise.

Living heritage

UNESCO describes living heritage as both inherited and contemporary: it can provide identity, continuity, and belonging because people keep recreating it. Its importance lies not simply in a cultural product, but in the knowledge, skills, and meaning carried through transmission.

“Maize is sacred to us because it connects us with our ancestors. It feeds our spirit as well as our bodies.”

— Juana Batz Puac, K’iche’ Maya Day Keeper, in the National Museum of the American Indian’s Living Maya Time

This named voice is not evidence for a universal theory and is not an input to the network. It keeps one distinction visible: a community’s account of what a narrative means differs from an analyst’s record of which motifs it contains.

If myths matter because they help people inhabit a world, what kinds of worlds do they make imaginable?

02

The worlds stories make

Water, sky, fire, first beings, death

Before a world can be inherited, it must be made intelligible.

Across the catalogue, land emerges from water, the sky once hangs close to the earth, fire enters human life, beings cross between animal and human forms, and death arrives through a delayed or broken message.

These stories do more than explain how something began. They make relationships visible: between people and landscape, kin and stranger, knowledge and danger, life and mortality. They turn elemental uncertainty into forms that can be remembered, argued with, and passed on.

They are not pieces of one original story. They are distinct narrative answers, carried by different communities through different languages, places, and histories.

A necessary distinction: primordial names the time imagined inside a narrative. It does not prove that a story can be historically dated or that distant versions descend from one ancient source.

The historical puzzle

When related stories appear across distant places, are we seeing contact, shared inheritance, adaptation to comparable environments, recurring human concerns—or some combination of them?

A network cannot answer that question by itself. It can show which patterns remain visible when the meaning of “similar” changes.
Water becomes land

Before there was ground

“Primeval waters,” “Earth grows big,” and earth-diver narratives sit within one of the clearest points of agreement between the two readings. Across all 926 records, those titles appear in 291, 277, and 146 entries respectively. Their recurrence matters inside this archive; it does not prove a universal beginning.

Earth is given a structure

When sky and earth drew apart

Another broad profile gathers the Sun and Moon, eclipses, a sky once close to earth, and images of the world held in place. These narratives imagine order, distance, orientation, and the limits of human reach.

Relations become social worlds

Kin, animals, and transformation

Marriage, overheard secrets, animal spouses, and tricksters such as fox, jackal, or coyote turn relations into consequences. They ask who belongs, what knowledge may be shared, and what happens when trust breaks.

Life enters human hands

Fire, plants, and emergence

Stories of stolen fire, food trees, first beings, and people ascending from an underworld place human life inside a living earth. Trees, rocks, caves, animals, and flame act within a moral and material world.

Mortality acquires a cause

When a message went wrong

In the smallest thematic profile, death often enters through delay, deception, or a muddled message. An irreversible fact becomes an event with agents and consequences—something a story can grieve, question, and pass on.

These five fields summarize patterns inferred from English catalogue titles and descriptions. They are analytical emphases, not five kinds of people, five stages of thought, or community-authored categories.

Stories endure not by remaining untouched, but by entering new voices, materials, occasions, and relationships.

03

Stories survive by changing

Transmission is transformation

A living tradition is never exactly the same twice.

Every telling carries a past into a new present. Variation is not automatically damage; it is often evidence that a narrative remains in use. Today that renewal unfolds alongside migration, urbanization, language shift, broadcast, and digital circulation—changes that can create new audiences while disrupting older settings of transmission.

Creative renewal

Change can be the life of tradition.

The 2003 UNESCO Convention describes living heritage as “constantly recreated by communities and groups in response to their environment, their interaction with nature and their history.” A performer adapts to an audience; a family retells for a new generation; a community gives an inherited form new work to do.

Fragile transmission

Change can also follow interruption.

UNESCO’s guidance on oral traditions identifies migration, urbanization, language loss, environmental change, and mass media as pressures that can reduce the spaces where knowledge passes person to person. The danger is not simply that wording changes, but that relationships of transmission become harder to sustain.

A story moving through today’s media

A named voice, a video, two languages, a transcript.

The National Museum of the American Indian’s bilingual Living Maya Time page places Juana Batz Puac’s K’iche’ Maya account alongside video and a downloadable transcript. The webpage does not replace living transmission. It shows one way digital mediation can widen access while keeping a speaker, language, and institutional source visible.

A story has more than one body

Clay, paint, print, voice, screen.

A narrative spoken in one language is not quite the same object when it is inscribed, translated, painted, printed, indexed, or rendered as searchable data. These historical objects show that mediation long predates the internet.

Reach is not the same as understanding

Technology can extend transmission—and thin its context.

Audio, video, broadcast, and digital media can help communities preserve and circulate expressive culture. But a narrative may become easier to find at the same moment that its language, occasion, teller, or cultural authority becomes harder to see.

When transmission becomes fragile, communities and institutions often turn to recordings, inventories, archives, and catalogues. These can keep cultural traces findable. They are not the living tradition itself.

04

Preservation makes an archive

Memory made findable

One way institutions preserve selected traces is by making records searchable.

Recordings, transcriptions, photographs, translations, and indexes can outlive an encounter. They allow later generations to find dispersed material and permit questions that no single reader could answer unaided.

Documentation can support preservation, but it cannot replace the everyday social life of a tradition. UNESCO emphasizes maintaining person-to-person transmission and safeguarding oral traditions as living processes—not freezing them as products.

A catalogue serves a different purpose. It keeps selected traces retrievable and comparable. That value is real: without records, many encounters would be unreachable to later listeners and researchers. The Berezkin catalogue belongs to this longer history of making recurrence searchable.

05

Every catalogue casts a shadow

The price of making things comparable

To catalogue is to choose a name, a unit, a language, and a boundary.

Classification makes selected traces retrievable by reducing variation to named features. That reduction makes comparison possible. The unintended risk begins when a selected trace is mistaken for the whole tradition: what the category cannot hold can disappear from view.

Washington, D.C. · 1916

The caption changed.

This photograph was once described as Densmore recording Mountain Chief. The Library of Congress later corrected the record: the horn is for playback, and Densmore’s own annotation said they were listening.

The correction is not a footnote to the image’s history. It is part of that history. Archives do not merely preserve encounters; names, titles, translations, descriptions, and silences shape what later viewers believe they can see.

“Any historical narrative is a particular bundle of silences.”

— Michel-Rolph Trouillot, Silencing the Past

What a category makes visible

Classification is useful because it is selective.

A motif index can gather hundreds of dispersed records around “Primeval waters” or “Theft of fire.” That shared label allows recurrence to be studied across a collection. It is a powerful scholarly instrument precisely because it does not try to reproduce every story in full.

“Each standard and each category valorizes some point of view and silences another.”

— Geoffrey C. Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, Sorting Things Out

What a category cannot hold

A motif is a trace, not the story itself.

A short title cannot preserve tone, gesture, audience, disagreement, sacred restriction, or the teller’s reason for speaking. Translation and editorial description also enter the record. What becomes comparable is therefore never identical to the source narrative or its performance and publication context.

“It is normally impossible to reconstitute the content of any real text on the basis of the set of motifs extracted.”

— Yuri Berezkin, “Folklore and Mythology Catalogue”

This study does not compare 926 complete cultures. It compares 926 records shaped, to varying degrees, by collection, translation, editing, and classification. Each is a trace of a much larger narrative world.

A blank cell means only that a motif was not recorded in this export; it does not prove that a community lacks the story. A coordinate marks where the catalogue places a record, not where a tradition began or the boundaries of a people. The second reading also depends on English titles and descriptions, so editorial language becomes part of what the method can see.

The networks organize an archive. They do not classify cultures themselves.

More about the frozen archive

The release contains 68,837 recorded motif presences and uses sixteen broad geographic regions for auditing. Of 2,138 motif documents, 2,052 include an English gloss and 86 do not. The source is the Berezkin–Duvakin Electronic Analytical Catalogue, with an English-facing interface at Maps of Myths.

These limits are not a reason to abandon comparison. They are a reason to compare reflexively: which patterns remain visible when the way similarity is recognized changes?

06

One catalogue, two ways of noticing

The same library, read twice

What changes when the meaning of “similar” changes?

Imagine reading the same library twice. On the first pass, resemblance is literal: two records move closer when they contain the same named catalogue motif, such as “Primeval sky close to earth” or “Theft of fire.” This reading protects the grain of recorded detail.

On the second pass, the view widens. Records are compared by their overall balance of themes—water and conflict, sky and world structure, kinship and transformation, first beings and landscape, mortality and deception. This reading can notice thematic echoes that exact labels miss.

Both begin with the same 926 records. The first asks, Do the same recorded elements recur? The second asks, Do different elements participate in a related thematic world? Neither is the archive’s one true shape. Each makes some relationships vivid and others faint.

Representation is not merely preparation. It helps decide what cultural similarity can look like.

What the broader reading gathers

Five constellations of emphasis

The themes introduced at the beginning reappear here as patterns in the English catalogue language. The largest profile emphasizes water, aquatic beings, heroes, monsters, and conflict. A second leans toward the Sun, Moon, eclipses, and the architecture of the world. A third brings kinship beside animal transformation. A fourth gathers first beings, plants, trees, rocks, climbing, fire, and emergence. The smallest concentrates origins of death and deceptive tricksters.

These are best read as overlapping emphases. Every group contains many other themes, and none names a people or worldview.

Validation boundary

The exact partition is highly stable across optimizer seeds. The thematic partition is less stable and its retrieval diagnostic falls below a popularity baseline, so T1–T5 remain provisional pending human semantic review.

The two pipelines hold the records and source catalogue constant, but not the feature space, similarity measure, graph rule, or grouping setting. Their differences belong to the full comparison pipeline—not to text modeling alone.

If the two readings produced entirely different structures, the archive would have no stable shape. If they matched perfectly, the choice of lens would make little difference. The result lies between those extremes.

07

What the readings share

Agreement without equivalence

A shared shape survives the change of lens.

If the two readings were unrelated, their groups would overlap little beyond chance. If they were interchangeable, their boundaries would match. The result falls between those extremes.

Three large regions of the archive remain recognizable when the lens changes. The largest exact-motif group is the exception: most of it divides between two broader thematic profiles. This is the central result. The readings agree too much to be unrelated, but not enough to be interchangeable.

The overlap is stronger than chance. None of 99,999 controlled shuffles reproduced as much agreement as the real data. Even when records were shuffled only within broad geographic regions, the observed agreement remained higher than expected. Broad regional composition supplies an important baseline, but it does not fully account for the shared structure.

The archive does not hold one shape. It holds a nonrandom backbone—and a fault line.
How the overlap was tested

Chance-adjusted pair agreement is ARI 0.3549; chance-adjusted shared information is AMI 0.3888; NMI is 0.3916; and variation of information is 2.5226 bits. No fixed-margin permutation among 99,999 draws reached the observed agreement (Monte Carlo p = 0.00001). A broad-region-restricted null raised mean ARI to 0.2075, yet the observed value remained higher (standardized contrast 17.52; p = 0.00001).

08

Where the readings part

The principal divergence

Where one exact-motif group becomes two thematic worlds

The largest exact-motif group contains 363 records. Under the broader reading, 162 move principally toward one profile and 148 toward another; the remaining 53 are distributed across the other three thematic profiles. What exact recurrence held together, thematic composition pulls apart.

One branch looks upward

Sky, light, and the order of the cosmos

Its strongest relative emphases involve the Sun and Moon, eclipses, and relations among sky, earth, and world. Narratives such as “Male sun and female moon” or “Primeval sky close to earth” turn the heavens into a field of relation: gendered, inhabited, dangerous, ordered, or transformed.

The other stays nearer the ground

First beings, landscape, and terrestrial life

This branch leans more strongly toward first people, plants and beings; trees, rocks and climbing; underworld emergence; and the passage of fire into human life. The earth is not an inert stage. It is a place from which beings emerge and with which they remain in relation.

These are not “celestial peoples” and “terrestrial peoples.” Both branches contain stories of sky and earth. They are provisional differences of emphasis created from English catalogue language—not identities, civilizations, or stages of human thought.

A dramatic list becomes a cautious conclusion

Individual motifs weakened. The broader contrast endured.

At first, 232 individual motifs appeared to distinguish the two branches. Once records were compared within broad regions, only eight remained. After also accounting for how unevenly the records were documented, none remained. The striking list of individual motifs was not robust.

What persisted was a broader contrast among four themes. The archive therefore supports a difference in thematic emphasis, not a universal division between kinds of stories or peoples. This is precisely why the two readings matter: stable themes and fragile individual details answer different questions.

Exact robustness sequence

232 motifs pass an unrestricted false-discovery screen; eight remain in broad-region-restricted permutations; zero remain after simultaneously restricting by broad region and recorded motif count. The four aggregate theme contrasts remain significant under all three controls.

Place enters the pattern

The thematic difference is also geographically uneven.

The sky-and-world-structure branch extends across several regions. The more terrestrial-leaning branch is much more concentrated: 90 of its 148 records are assigned by the catalogue to Amazonia or Northern South America. That concentration is substantial within this dataset, but it cannot be turned into an origin story.

The two branches are not documented equally. Records in one contain an average of 35.3 motifs; records in the other contain 61.1. A fuller catalogue record offers more opportunities to match other records. It must never be mistaken for evidence that one culture has richer, more complex, or more creative traditions.

The language evidence is incomplete as well: family information is missing for 340 of the 926 records, and the remaining labels are too varied to support a simple ancestry claim. Environment matters to human life, but the climate available here is modern and attached to approximate reference locations. Temperature, rainfall, and latitude can describe the archive’s present spatial pattern; they cannot reconstruct the conditions under which a narrative arose, traveled, or changed.

09

Neither universal nor alone

What the shape of myth means

Similarity without sameness. Difference without isolation.

Myths can do more than describe nature; they can make relationships with it thinkable. They turn sky and water, birth and death, animals and kin, danger and obligation into forms people can remember, perform, debate, and pass on.

This study resists two damaging simplifications. The first is homogenization: treating culturally distinct narratives as interchangeable versions of one universal story. The second is fragmentation: treating every tradition as sealed from every other and denying the recurrence of human questions about origin, mortality, kinship, nature, danger, and transformation.

The paired reading makes both truths visible. Exact motifs protect the grain of recorded difference. Broader themes reveal echoes that exact labels can miss. Across this archive, recurring concerns become visible—but never outside particular languages, landscapes, histories, and systems of knowledge.

As a methodological implication—not a measured result of this study—this matters wherever museums, archives, classrooms, search systems, and AI systems decide what counts as “the same.” A system that merges related language too readily can flatten distinct knowledge traditions into generic content. A system that recognizes only exact labels can miss meaningful relationships. Responsible comparison should reveal the lens it used, preserve uncertainty, and return questions of meaning and authority to the people whose knowledge is being represented.

None of this proves migration, borrowing, common ancestry, an original homeland, or environmental cause. The networks show where independent historical, linguistic, archaeological, ecological, ethnographic, and community-grounded research should listen next.

People make worlds by living in them and again by telling them. A catalogue makes a third world: a world of comparable traces. That world can reveal patterns no single reader could hold in mind—but it must never be mistaken for the lives from which it was made.

From access to authority

“Indigenous nations and communities must be the ones to determine data governance protocols.”

— Stephanie Russo Carroll and colleagues, CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance

Public-domain status and online access answer a copyright question. They do not erase cultural authority. Future work should pair computational comparison with human semantic review, community consultation where invited, careful historical chronology, and source-specific stewardship.

The networks show where to listen next. They do not decide what a story means.
