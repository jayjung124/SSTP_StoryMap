A history of stories, archives, and comparison

The Shape of Myth

How stories became records, how records became networks, and what survives when the same archive is read two ways.

Yaejoon (Jay) Jung · Independent research project

Prologue

Before the database

A story happens between people

“Folklore is the boiled-down juice of human living.”

— Zora Neale Hurston, “Folklore and Music”

Before a story becomes data, it is an encounter.

Stories do not enter the world as rows in a table. They are spoken, sung, painted, printed, translated, remembered, contested, and retold. They move through bodies, places, materials, and relationships.

To call something a myth is already to take a comparative position. Communities may speak instead of sacred histories, teachings, tales, songs, genealogies, ancestral accounts, or forms named in their own languages. This project retains the catalogue’s terminology so its analysis can be audited; it does not presume that “myth” is every community’s own category.

Smithsonian Folklife describes folklore and folklife as expressive culture learned orally, by imitation, or in performance. The emphasis belongs on culture in use, not on a detachable plot alone.

Living heritage

The 2003 UNESCO Convention describes intangible cultural heritage as “constantly recreated by communities and groups in response to their environment, their interaction with nature and their history.” Recurrence therefore need not mean a frozen text copied unchanged across time.

“Maize is sacred to us because it connects us with our ancestors. It feeds our spirit as well as our bodies.”

— Juana Batz Puac, K’iche’ Maya Day Keeper, in the National Museum of the American Indian’s Living Maya Time

This named voice is not evidence for a universal theory and is not an input to the network. It appears here to keep one distinction visible: a community’s account of what a narrative means is different from an analyst’s record of what motifs it contains.

01

Forms of transmission

Clay, paint, print, voice

Stories change medium without becoming the same thing.

A narrative may live in speech, ceremony, clay, manuscript, vernacular poetry, popular print, or digital media. These are not stages in a single march from “oral” to “modern.” They are different historical conditions of transmission, and they continue to coexist.

These objects are historical counterpoints, not examples sampled from the 926 catalogue records and not representatives of any algorithmic community. Their role is to show what a motif matrix necessarily leaves outside its frame: voice, material, use, genre, audience, and authority.

02

From encounter to catalogue

A record is made, not found

When a voice becomes an archive

Between a performed narrative and a database row lie decisions about who listened, what was recorded, which language was used, how the encounter was described, and what later cataloguers chose to retrieve.

Washington, D.C. · 1916

The caption changed.

This photograph was once described as Densmore recording Mountain Chief. The Library of Congress later corrected the record: the horn is for playback, and Densmore’s own annotation said they were listening.

The correction is not a footnote to the image’s history. It is part of that history. Archives do not merely preserve encounters; titles, descriptions, names, translations, and silences shape what later viewers believe they can see.

“Any historical narrative is a particular bundle of silences.”

— Michel-Rolph Trouillot, Silencing the Past

A century of comparison

From tales to types, from motifs to matrices

Comparative folklorists have long created indexes to study recurrence. Each system made dispersed material comparable—and each reduced a more complex narrative life to selected features.

“Each standard and each category valorizes some point of view and silences another.”

— Geoffrey C. Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, Sorting Things Out

    Antti Aarne indexes folktale types

926 traces—not 926 cultural worlds

The source is the Berezkin–Duvakin Electronic Analytical Catalogue of Folklore and Mythological Motifs, with an English-facing interface at Maps of Myths. A row is an analytical catalogue record, not a complete portrait of a living people, religion, or narrative tradition.

A matrix zero means not recorded in this export. It does not prove cultural absence. Coordinates are reference locations attached to records—not origins, homelands, territories, or routes of transmission.

English wording enters the instrument

The semantic reading depends on English motif titles and glosses. Of 2,138 motif documents, 2,052 include a gloss and 86 do not. Translation, editorial phrasing, missing description, and catalogue design therefore participate in what the model can see.

These networks organize a recorded catalogue. They do not classify cultures themselves.

03

The paired reading

Same records, different questions

Read the archive twice.

The first reading listens for exact recurrence: which named motif codes appear together? The second listens at a broader level: which records have similarly composed profiles of English-language themes?

Exact recurrence

A shared code counts as a match.

Each catalogue record becomes a repertoire of 2,138 possible motif presences. Two records resemble one another when they share specific recorded motifs relative to the motifs recorded in either.

It asks: Do the same catalogue elements recur?

same926records
Thematic composition

Related wording can count as resemblance.

Motif titles and glosses become fifteen broad semantic factors. Each record is summarized by its mixture of those factors, then compared by the shape of that mixture.

It asks: Do broader fields of meaning recur?

If representation were only a neutral container, the two readings would arrange the archive in the same way. They do not. Yet they do not produce unrelated worlds either.

How the two readings were constructed

Exact-motif graph

Ordinary Jaccard similarity; all positive off-diagonal ties retained; 358,966 weighted edges; weighted Leiden at γ = 1.00; four communities.

Semantic graph

Sublinear L2-normalized TF–IDF; 15 NMF factors; cosine similarity; undirected union-kNN at k = 12; 8,854 weighted edges; weighted Leiden at γ = 0.75; five provisional communities.

Validation boundary

Mean optimizer-seed ARI is 0.9933 for the exact partition and 0.8581 for the semantic partition. The semantic model’s NDCG@50 diagnostic is 0.0276, below a 0.1417 popularity baseline; T1–T5 remain provisional pending human semantic validation.

Both pipelines hold the nodes and source catalogue constant, but not the feature space, similarity measure, graph rule, or Leiden setting. Differences belong to the full representation-and-graph pipeline—not to text modeling alone.

Among 428,275 unordered record pairs, 55,389 are grouped together by both partitions, 66,097 only by the exact partition, 38,473 only by the semantic partition, and 268,316 by neither. The raw Rand index of 0.7558 is elevated by this large jointly separated category; it is not “76% recovery.”

No fixed-margin permutation among 99,999 draws reaches the observed agreement (analytical z-Rand 151.11; Monte Carlo p = 0.00001). A macroarea-restricted null raises mean ARI to 0.2075, yet observed ARI remains higher (standardized contrast 17.52; p = 0.00001). Broad geography supplies a substantial baseline but does not exhaust the alignment.

The principal divergence

One exact-motif community, two semantic profiles

Where the archive changes shape

The largest exact-motif community contains 363 records. Read semantically, 162 enter T2 and 148 enter T4; the remaining 53 enter T1, T3, or T5. What exact recurrence held together, broader thematic composition divides.

This is not a division between “celestial peoples” and “terrestrial peoples.” It is a contrast between provisional profiles produced from English catalogue wording. The archive’s shadow

What the pattern travels with

Every archive casts a shadow.

Geography, translation, language metadata, and documentation density do not sit outside the model. They shape which similarities can become legible and how cautiously those similarities can be interpreted.

Only 586 of 926 records have a nonblank family label. Ninety-seven observed labels cannot support a simple ancestry claim.

Interpretive boundaryDepth ≠ richness

Recorded motif count indexes catalogue documentation. It is not a measure of a culture’s narrative complexity, creativity, or importance.

07

Coda

A bounded conclusion

Neither sameness nor isolation

Similarity can mean recurrence without equivalence. Difference can mean cultural specificity without isolation. Across these 926 records, two ways of reading the same mediated archive preserve a nonrandom backbone while reorganizing local relationships.

This does not prove migration, borrowing, common ancestry, origin, or environmental cause. It tells us where independent historical, linguistic, archaeological, ecological, ethnographic, and community-grounded evidence is now needed.

A network can show where an archive holds together and where it changes shape. History must decide why.

The analysis supports

  • nonrandom but incomplete agreement;
  • three prominent one-to-one anchors;
  • a principal B1 subdivision into B1∩T2 and B1∩T4;
  • a persistent aggregate thematic contrast;
  • substantial covariation with geography and documentation;
  • representation and graph construction as substantive research choices.

The analysis does not establish

  • migration, borrowing, or transmission direction;
  • common ancestry or a date of divergence;
  • where a motif originated;
  • an environmental cause;
  • emic cultural categories;
  • the superiority of topic modeling;
  • a representative sample of all traditions or performances.

From access to authority

“Indigenous nations and communities must be the ones to determine data governance protocols.”

— Stephanie Russo Carroll and colleagues, CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance

Public-domain status and online access answer a copyright question. They do not erase cultural authority. Future work should pair computational comparison with human semantic review, community consultation where invited, careful historical chronology, and source-specific stewardship.

What the next historical test requires
  1. Human semantic review: area specialists, translators, folklorists, and—where feasible and invited—knowledge holders review wording, labels, and representative records.
  2. Chronology beyond the network: narrow motif families are tested against independent dates, movements, settlements, and exchange evidence.
  3. Language-aware models: missingness, genealogy, contact, translation, and multilingual naming are modeled explicitly.
  4. Spatial and ecological alternatives: null models preserve local autocorrelation and sampling density; environmental claims require dated mechanisms.
  5. Documentation sensitivity: repertoire matching, source blocking, and down-sampling test which structures depend on catalogue depth.
  6. Model uncertainty: factor number, random seeds, graph rules, Leiden resolution, and translation choices are propagated through the comparison.
