Across 926 catalogue records, two ways of recognizing resemblance reveal the same broad backbone—and a consequential difference in what comes into view.

01 · What myth means

Myth helps people live inside a larger world.

Myths are not only stories about a distant past. In different communities, narratives that scholars group under the word myth may be sacred histories, teachings, genealogies, songs, warnings, or forms named in their own languages.

They can orient people toward landscapes and ancestors, carry memory and belonging, and make questions of origin, obligation, kinship, suffering, and mortality thinkable. No single function belongs to every story. Meaning depends on who tells it, who may hear it, when it is performed, and what relationships it sustains.

“Folklore is the boiled-down juice of human living.”

— Zora Neale Hurston, writing about folklore in “Folklore and Music”

02 · The value of myth

Stories give elemental questions a form.

Across the catalogue, recurring narratives make relationships among people, nature, knowledge, danger, and mortality visible.

Land from water
Primeval waters and earth-diver stories imagine how ground becomes possible.
Sky and world-order
The Sun, Moon, eclipses, and a sky once close to earth make cosmic order narratable.
Kinship and transformation
Animal spouses, overheard secrets, and tricksters test belonging, trust, and change.
Fire and first beings
Stolen fire, first people, plants, rocks, and emergence place human life inside a living earth.
The origin of death
Delayed, altered, or broken messages turn mortality into an event that can be questioned and mourned.

Boundary: these are recurring emphases in English catalogue descriptions—not one original story, universal stages of thought, or community-authored categories.

03 · How myth is changing

Living traditions survive by changing.

Every telling carries a past into a new present. Variation is not automatically damage; it is often evidence that a narrative remains in use.

Today, migration, language shift, broadcast media, and digital circulation can bring stories to new audiences. They can also thin context. A narrative may become easier to find while its language, occasion, teller, or cultural authority becomes harder to see.

Wider reach is therefore not the same as fuller understanding. The question is not whether stories change, but who shapes that change, what travels with them, and what is left behind.

04 · Preservation and cataloguing

When transmission becomes fragile, selected traces become records.

Recordings, transcriptions, translations, indexes, and databases can keep dispersed material retrievable. They support preservation by making selected traces searchable; they do not replace the social life of a tradition.

Comparative folklore developed systems for finding recurring tale types and motifs across collections. The Berezkin–Duvakin Electronic Analytical Catalogue extends that lineage in digital form. This study analyzes one frozen export: 926 catalogue records represented through 2,138 recorded motif variables.

05 · The unintended cost

To classify is to choose a name, a unit, a language, and a boundary.

Classification makes comparison possible by reducing variation to retrievable features. Its power is also its limit.

A motif title cannot preserve tone, gesture, audience, disagreement, sacred restriction, or a teller’s reason for speaking. A blank cell means only that a motif was not recorded in this export. It does not prove that a community lacks the narrative. English descriptions and uneven documentation also shape what a computational reading can see.

“Each standard and each category valorizes some point of view and silences another.”

— Geoffrey C. Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, Sorting Things Out

Boundary: these networks organize records in an archive. They do not classify cultures, and a motif is a trace—not the story itself.

06 · The findings

One archive, two readings, one revealing fault line.

The same 926 records were organized through two complete analytical lenses. The first recognizes overlap in exact recorded motif codes. The second recognizes broader thematic profiles inferred from English motif titles and descriptions.

The two readings preserve a shared, nonrandom backbone within the catalogue: three large regions of the archive remain recognizable when the lens changes. But they are not interchangeable. The largest exact-motif group divides principally between two thematic emphases—one oriented toward the Sun, Moon, sky, and world-order; the other toward first people, plants and beings, trees, rocks, and terrestrial life.

The most dramatic individual-motif differences weaken after broad geography and uneven documentation are taken into account. The broader thematic contrast persists. The defensible conclusion is therefore a difference in emphasis—not a division between kinds of peoples or stages of human thought.

Boundary: the networks identify patterns that require explanation. They do not prove migration, borrowing, common ancestry, environmental cause, or an original homeland.

Explore further: theme contrasts, geographic composition, group profiles, climate context, and latitude distributions.

07 · What it means for society

Similarity without sameness. Difference without isolation.

This study resists two damaging simplifications.

Homogenization turns culturally distinct narratives into interchangeable versions of one universal story. Fragmentation treats every tradition as sealed from every other and makes recurring human questions impossible to recognize.

The paired reading keeps both truths in view. Exact motifs protect the grain of recorded difference. Broader themes reveal echoes that exact labels can miss. Neither should stand alone.

This is a methodological implication, not a measured result about present-day technology: archives, search systems, classrooms, and AI systems all make decisions about what counts as “the same.” Responsible comparison should identify its lens, preserve provenance and uncertainty, and return questions of meaning and authority to the communities whose knowledge is being represented.

The networks show where to listen next. They do not decide what a story means.
