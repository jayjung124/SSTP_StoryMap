01

The recurrence question

A controlled comparison

When stories recur, what kind of similarity are we seeing?

A resemblance is not an explanation

Across a large comparative catalogue, related images, actions, beings, and narrative problems recur in records associated with distant communities. A resemblance may reflect historical contact, movement, shared ancestry, parallel response to an environment, recurring human concerns, a collector’s categories, uneven documentation, or several of these at once.

The resemblance alone cannot choose among them.

Change the measurement

This study holds the same 926 catalogue records constant and compares them twice: first through exact recorded-motif co-presence, then through broader semantic profiles. The question is which community patterns remain recognizable—and where the archive reorganizes.

The result in one sentence

Myths are neither simply universal nor purely local. Their recorded similarities remain recognizable at one scale and reorganize at another.

The archive

Recorded evidence, mediated evidence

What this archive is—and is not

The source is the Berezkin–Duvakin Electronic Analytical Catalogue of Folklore and Mythological Motifs, with an English-facing interface at Maps of Myths. The frozen analytical release contains 926 catalogue records, 2,138 motif variables, and 68,837 recorded tradition–motif presences.

Analytical units, not cultural wholes

A catalogue record is not a complete portrait of a living people, religion, or narrative tradition. Some labels aggregate named groups; some may be outdated or exonymic. A motif is a coded recurrence—not a full performance or a storyteller’s exact words.

A matrix zero means not recorded here. It should not automatically be treated as proof that a motif is culturally absent.

Language enters the measurement

The semantic pipeline depends on English motif titles and glosses. Of 2,138 motif documents, 2,052 include a gloss and 86 do not. One of those 86, m29g1_9, also lacks a descriptive title in the frozen dictionary and is shown transparently as missing rather than guessed.

Translation, editorial phrasing, documentation density, and catalogue design are therefore part of the research instrument.

These networks organize a recorded catalogue. They do not classify cultures themselves.

Ethical position

The public presentation draws guidance from Smithsonian Folklife work on cultural sustainability and shared stewardship: name provenance, preserve uncertainty, avoid cultural essences, and distinguish access from authority.

This remains secondary archive analysis—not community-led documentation or co-curation—and does not imply Smithsonian affiliation or endorsement.

Nonrandom—and incomplete

Pair structure

Among 428,275 unordered tradition pairs, 55,389 are grouped together by both partitions, 66,097 only by the binary partition, 38,473 only by the semantic partition, and 268,316 by neither.

The raw Rand index is 0.7558, but most pairs contribute because both partitions separate them. It is not “76% recovery.”

Null models

No fixed-margin permutation among 99,999 draws reaches the observed agreement (z-Rand 151.11; p = 0.00001). A macroarea-restricted null raises mean ARI to 0.2075, yet observed ARI remains higher (z = 17.52; p = 0.00001).

Broad geography supplies a substantial baseline but does not exhaust the shared structure.

Principal divergence

One community, two semantic profiles

The largest binary community reorganizes

Binary community B1 contains 363 records. Under the semantic partition, 162 enter T2 and 148 enter T4; the remaining 53 enter T1, T3, or T5. The two main branches account for 85.4% of B1—but neither is a complete topic community.

Aggregate themes persist more clearly than individual motifs

Across 2,138 motifs, 232 differences pass an unstratified Benjamini–Hochberg screen. Eight survive macroarea restriction, all favoring T4. None survives simultaneous macroarea × recorded-repertoire-quartile restriction. Individual motif contrasts are therefore highly context-sensitive even while the aggregate factor composition persists.

90 of 148

B1∩T4 records are assigned to Amazonia and Northern South America (60.8%). Across the twelve represented macroareas, χ²(11) = 94.53, permutation p = 0.00001, Cramér’s V = 0.552, and Jensen–Shannon distance = 0.496.

Interpretation changes with context

Geography, documentation, language, and environment

The right question is not whether context “explains away” the result, but how much contextual structure already enters what the semantic pipeline can resolve.

Interpretive boundary

Depth is not richness

Recorded motif count indexes catalogue documentation. It is not a measure of a culture’s narrative complexity or importance.

07

Meaning

A bounded conclusion

Neither universal sameness nor absolute isolation

Exact recorded-motif co-presence and semantic-profile similarity preserve a nonrandom shared backbone while reorganizing local membership. Three prominent anchors persist, and the largest binary community divides into two semantically coherent—but context-entangled—branches.

The analysis supports

  • nonrandom but incomplete agreement;
  • three prominent one-to-one anchors;
  • a principal B1 subdivision into B1∩T2 and B1∩T4;
  • a persistent aggregate thematic contrast;
  • substantial covariation with geography and documentation;
  • representation and graph construction as substantive research choices.

The analysis does not establish

  • migration, borrowing, or transmission direction;
  • common ancestry or a date of divergence;
  • where a motif originated;
  • an environmental cause;
  • emic cultural categories;
  • the superiority of topic modeling;
  • a representative sample of all traditions or performances.
The two methods observe different levels of the same recorded archive. Their disagreement is evidence about the measurement architecture.

Next research direction

From structural result to historical test

  1. Human semantic validationArea specialists, translators, folklorists, and—where feasible and invited—knowledge holders review wording, factor labels, and representative records.
  2. Historical and archaeological chronologyNarrow motif families are tested against independent dates, movements, settlements, and exchange evidence.
  3. Language-aware modelsMissingness, relatedness, genealogy, contact, and multilingual naming are modeled explicitly.
  4. Spatial and ecological alternativesNulls preserve local autocorrelation and sampling density; environmental variables require dated mechanisms.
  5. Documentation sensitivityRepertoire matching, source blocking, and down-sampling test which structures depend on catalogue depth.
  6. Model uncertaintyNMF seeds, factor number, graph rules, Leiden resolution, and translation choices are propagated through the comparison.
